## 1. What is OMBS?

OMBS is an open standard for recognizing evidence of student agency developed through making and building practices. It provides shared language for recognizing and recording these practices. It is not a curriculum, a rubric, or an assessment. It does not prescribe what young people must build or learn.

Educators and tool builders translate OMBS into learning experiences and assessment instruments suited to their context. OMBS seeks to gather evidence to help educators recognize students’ progress and to provide relevant feedback. Over time, this evidence becomes a record of learning and growth.

The goal is for a project made with a construction kit, a coding application, a robotics program, a school assignment, or materials found at home to produce evidence that can be understood through the same framework. Products and learning environments can remain different. The language of demonstrated maker agency can be shared.

### 1.1 Designing for maker agency

The Universal Design for Learning (UDL) defines learner agency as “agency that is purposeful & reflective, resourceful & authentic, strategic & action-oriented” (CAST, 2024a). We define maker agency as learner agency as seen within making and building practices:

- **Making is an activity in which the learner constructs an artifact that others can experience, and that offers the learner feedback.** A learner imagines something that does not yet exist. They confront the gap between their intention and reality. They ask questions, seek resources, work with people and tools, test ideas, encounter failure, revise their thinking, and eventually present something they can explain and defend.

- **Maker practices and OMBS cross subject areas, mediums, and pedagogy.** A learner’s artifact may be physical, digital, performative, written, social, or a mix. Making is a practice observable across spaces, not a property of a particular tool or room. A practice can show in a classroom, a kitchen, a community center, or a fab lab.

- **A making activity has a clear goal and an audience.** The goal may be functional, that the artifact works under real conditions, or human, that the artifact fits the cultural practices of the people it is made for.

OMBS describes what agency looks like when learners make things and what evidence shows it. The Design Guide shows how learning experiences can be designed to promote maker agency. *See: Design Guide (in preparation).*

### 1.2 OMBS commitments

OMBS seeks to develop student agency: a learner's increasing ability to move through uncertainty and bring an intention into the world. It will treat maker agency as a capacity that develops through experience and practice, not as a personality trait someone either possesses or lacks.

In looking for evidence of maker agency, OMBS will not standardize imagination or confuse a polished output with understanding. It will not treat help, collaboration, or AI use as a lack of agency. Five commitments about how OMBS tracks evidence follow from this:

1. **Practice over outcome.** OMBS describes what learners do, not what they produce. A polished artifact made through copying shows less practice evidence than a rough artifact designed intentionally.

2. **Medium-agnostic.** The same practice applies whether the artifact is an art piece, a circuit, a community event, or a game world.

3. **Process-visible.** OMBS recognizes learners who make their thinking observable: drafts kept, choices defended, revisions documented. A standard that can only be assessed from the final artifact is a standard that rewards luck and home support.

4. **Context-aware.** OMBS does not evaluate an artifact in isolation. It captures the relationship among the learner's intention; the actions they took; the decisions they owned; the support and tools they used; the evidence they examined; and the audience they shared with.

5. **Iteration-positive.** Revising a solution based on testing, feedback, or new information is a core maker practice. OMBS recognizes evidence of revisions informed by evidence.

### 1.3 Who uses OMBS

OMBS is for schools, families, makerspaces, learning organizations, companies, and digital platforms.

- **Schools** plan project-based units, observe and document practice, and report to families. They can also understand learning that occurred outside their walls.

- **Families** can recognize growth that grades do not capture. They can read the practices to understand what making looks like, and they can make claims about work they guided or observed at home.

- **Makerspaces** describe what learners demonstrate across programs in one language.

- **Learning organizations** can improve the experiences they design.

- **Companies** can show that their products support genuine development.

- **Digital platforms** connect claims to evidence and report to learners and families in language they can read.

### 1.4 What OMBS contains

- **Maker practices.** What types of actions the student takes.

- **Growth over time.** The shifts across a student’s body of work that reports should support.

- **Report structure.** The claim, the evidence, the context, and the interpretation.

- **Authorship language.** How to specify ownership of a decision or action.

**Connection to the design guide.** OMBS gives structure between an open project, which is hard to start, and a prescribed project, which limits the learner. The design guide carries the full treatment and gives worked examples by kind of making. *See: Design Guide (in preparation).*

## 2. Maker practices

OMBS uses five maker practices to describe what the learner does when they make something, and how their capacity develops over time. These practices are not stages and do not need to occur in order. Learners move among them repeatedly.

Any making experience can be read through the same questions. What did the learner define? How did they investigate? What did they make? How did they iterate? What can they share and carry forward?

| Practice | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Define | Setting the goal; the criteria and constraints applied to it. |
| Investigate | Asking questions, researching, judging which information to trust, seeking help. |
| Make | Developing solutions; producing an artifact. |
| Iterate | Testing and feedback; iteration and revision. |
| Share | Explaining or presenting the work; making a representation of it; extending the work to future practice. |

The eight shifts that describe growth across a body of work are attached to these practices. They are listed under Growth over Time.

### 2.1 Define

The learner develops ownership of a direction. They notice a possibility, identify a need, form a question, imagine an outcome, or make meaningful choices within an existing prompt. The goal may change during the work.

**Shows as:**

- The learner names what they want to make and why it matters to them.

- The learner can say what was given and what they chose.

- The learner names the constraints they recognized.

- The learner says how they will know the work succeeded.

- The learner's intention changed during the work and they can say how.

- The goal comes from the learner's own interest, and they can say why it matters.

- The learner names an audience precisely enough that someone else could tell it from a neighboring one.

- The learner names the change the work should produce in that audience.

- The learner anticipates conditions of use, including misuse and time.

**Does not show as:**

- "I want to make something cool."

- The learner restates the prompt as the goal.

- "Everyone" as the audience.

### 2.2 Investigate

The learner finds what they need in order to move forward. They ask questions, research, experiment, examine examples, use tools, consult AI, or seek help from people with relevant knowledge. Seeking help is an expression of agency, not a reduction of it.

**Shows as:**

- The questions the learner asked.

- The sources, tools, and people the learner consulted, and how they judged which to trust.

- What the learner tried before asking for help.

- How new information changed the learner's approach.

- The learner asks a peer for help and can say what they took from it.

**Does not show as:**

- "I looked it up," with no account of what was found or what changed.

- The learner asks for the answer rather than asking a question that moves the work forward.

- A found solution or an AI output used without judgment of it.

### 2.3 Make

The learner turns an idea into something that can encounter reality. They construct, write, code, compose, combine, simulate, repair, or perform. The result does not need to be polished or complete. It needs to create feedback.

**Shows as:**

- Decisions the learner made, and how they responded to constraints.

- How the learner used materials and tools.

- How the learner turned the idea into a first attempt. Early versions are kept, not erased.

- Which parts the learner produced, directed, adapted, or delegated.

- The learner chose materials, tools, or a medium over named alternatives and can say why.

- The learner can say which parts of the work depend on which, and where it is fragile.

- The learner makes choices the medium allows and defends them as choices.

**Does not show as:**

- A polished artifact with no trace of the decisions behind it.

- A kit or set of steps followed with no choice made by the learner.

- Materials used "because they were there".

### 2.4 Iterate

The learner studies what happened and decides what to do next. They test, solicit feedback, identify discrepancies, compare alternatives, debug, reconsider assumptions, and revise. Iteration is not measured by counting versions. The adaptation need not be visible in the artifact alone. A trace, an account from another person, or the learner's account can show it.

**Shows as:**

- What changed between versions, why, and what evidence informed the change. "I cut the second example because three of five testers stopped reading there."

- The learner hands the work to someone or something whose response is uncertain, and watches what happens.

- How the learner responded to failure or surprise.

- Later attempts show learning from earlier ones.

- The learner engages a critique, identifies what it surfaces, and chooses what to act on, with reasons.

- The learner tests against the conditions of use they anticipated, including the worst plausible use.

- The learner puts the work in the hands of the intended user and observes what happens.

- The learner revises a material or design choice in response to a test.

- The learner gives critique to a peer in terms that serve the peer's work.

**Does not show as:**

- "I changed it because Mrs. Park said to."

- Many superficial revisions counted as more learning than two consequential ones.

- Changing whatever the critic said (compliance), or changing nothing and citing the critic's misunderstanding (defense).

- A test that cannot fail, such as walking gently across the bridge.

- Insisting the work "works" while the audience struggles with it.

### 2.5 Share

The learner makes their thinking available to other people and connects the experience to what comes next. They present the work, explain decisions, acknowledge assistance, answer questions, teach others, reflect on learning, and identify future possibilities.

**Shows as:**

- The learner explains how the work functions and justifies important choices.

- The learner can reconstruct their process and acknowledges who and what helped.

- The learner says what they learned, what remains unresolved, and what they would try next.

- The learner teaches a technique to another learner.

- The learner presents the work to an audience and responds to what the audience does or asks.

- The learner defends a formal choice by reference to purpose, not convention.

**Does not show as:**

- A summary written after the work that shows no usable understanding.

- Describing the artifact without the reasoning behind it.

- Presenting the work with no connection to what comes next.

## 3. Growth over time

A learner's evidence should accumulate over time. No single project needs to prove that a learner 'has agency.' A body of work can reveal an increasingly consistent capacity to act, learn, and continue.

OMBS does not reduce agency to a universal sequence of levels. Instead, it describes shifts that can appear across the maker practices. Each shift belongs to one practice.

| Practice | Shift |
|---|---|
| Define | From waiting for direction toward initiating action. |
| Investigate | From asking for answers toward asking productive questions. From using information toward evaluating it. |
| Make | From following a process toward making consequential choices. |
| Iterate | From reacting to failure toward learning from it. From making changes toward making evidence-informed changes. |
| Share | From describing an artifact toward explaining reasoning. From completing one project toward carrying capability into the next. |

These are signals, not rigid stages. A shift is read across a body of work, not within one claim. A reader compares claims about the same practice across projects and asks whether the later ones sit further along the shift than the earlier ones. No single claim carries a marker for a shift.

Growth is emergent rather than linear. A learner may demonstrate independence in one context and need substantial support in another.

Across a body of work, the boundary between what the learner does and what they hand off moves, and the learner steers it. The direction of travel is not prescribed. A learner who takes over a part they previously handed off shows growth. So does a learner who hands off a part they had been doing by hand and can say why that is now the right call. What is assessed is whether the learner is steering the boundary, not which side of it grows.

A body of work shows what occurred. A practice that does not appear may not have had an opportunity to appear. OMBS records nothing for an absence.

## 4. Report structure

An OMBS report connects the claim, the evidence, the context, and the interpretation. Together they should answer: **What does the making of this thing allow us to claim about the learner?**

- A report must be made by a reporter with standing to do so (see 4.5). An AI system cannot make a claim.
- Each piece of the report includes the author or authors who created that claim (4.1), evidence (4.2), context (4.3), or interpretation (4.4).
- Claim, evidence, context, and interpretation may be machine-generated, such as a transcript with a chatbot. In this case, the software is reported as the author.
- A software-suggested claim (4.1) or interpretation (4.4) is flagged as "suggested" until a teacher, coach, or other approved person confirms it.
- Correcting or otherwise changing a report after the fact is allowed but must be tracked. A history of edits, withdrawals, and deletions is recorded along with who made the change.

### 4.1 Claim

A claim answers the question: **What developing capability is being recognized?**

In OMBS a claim is about a practice, supported by evidence from the student's work. For example: "The learner adapted their approach in response to evidence from testing."

A claim may optionally be configured for an independent reviewer to check the claim against its evidence. An independent reviewer may state their disagreements, identify limitations, or ask for more evidence.

A claim may address work in a single project or across multiple projects. Claims across projects may address the learner's trajectory by comparing attempts and showing what the learner did, what help they used, and what changed.

The format in which a claim and its evidence are recorded and exchanged is defined in the tool guide.

### 4.2 Evidence

The evidence answers the question: **What can another person examine?**

Each piece of evidence submitted to support a claim also provides the creation date as well as the relevant context (4.3). This allows a claim to compare pieces of evidence from two different projects.

Evidence capture should occur naturally during the making process wherever possible, and should support learning rather than overwhelm it.

Any interaction data or digital artifacts captured as evidence should only be done with full transparency and with the consent of the learner and their parent or guardian. The learner or their guardian is able to correct or to withdraw evidence. OMBS does not ask for continuous recording.

1. **Artifacts.** The things the learner made in the project. An artifact may be physical, digital, performative, written, social, or a mix. Nobody made it as evidence; it is the work. A project almost always has several artifacts: the final piece, a kept draft, a prior version, a prototype, a test piece, a photograph of a build mid-assembly, a slide deck made to share the work. While a claim is in use, drafts are kept for the learner, not erased.

2. **Traces of the work.** Things made during the work that show what happened. Some are made by the learner: writing, a sketch, an annotation, a message, a saved revision, a spoken note. Some are made by the tools the learner used, without anyone choosing to make them: a timestamp, a version history, a submission event, a log of an AI conversation. Photographs or videos taken during the work are traces. A trace may help fix the order of events. A learner saying "I changed it partway through" does not establish when; a saved earlier version does.

3. **Others' accounts.** Records from people who observed, coached, or received the work. This may include an educator who coached the work; a peer, including a peer the learner taught or learned from; an audience or user; a family member. An account from someone who was present records what they saw: a teacher's note, a peer's log, a caption written by an adult who watched. An account from an audience or user records what they did or said when they met the work. It is distinct from the learner's account of how the audience reacted.

4. **The learner's account.** What the learner says about the work: a recorded explanation, a presentation, an oral defense, an interview answer, a written or spoken reflection. In it the learner explains their intentions, choices, assistance, failures, and discoveries. A slide deck made for a presentation is an artifact. What the learner says when presenting is the learner's account.

### 4.3 Context

Context answers the question: **Under what conditions was the evidence produced?**

The context describes the project that the evidence relates to. Each claim points to the parts of the context that bear on it and adds what is specific to that claim. For a team project the context is shared, and each claim names its contributor.

The context has four groups:

1. **Conditions.** What was prescribed and what resources were available to the learner.

2. **The learner's goal.** What the learner set out to make. A maker project requires the learner to set a goal for what they make, and the goal may change during the work. The context records the goal so that every claim about the project can be read against it. A change in the goal is itself evidence for Define.

3. **Contributions.** What people and tools contributed, and whether the work was an individual's or a team's. A contributor may be a person, a team, or a tool. When AI contributed to the work, it appears here.

4. **Ownership.** Which decisions belonged to the learner and how the learner made use of the help available. OMBS does not quantify how much help was used. It asks whether the learner remained the source of choice, and records the sources of help. For example, a claim about creating 3D models would need to include information about what the learner modeled from scratch vs. what they modified or used directly from another person's model or an AI-generated model.

### 4.4 Interpretation

Interpretation answers the question: **Why does this evidence support the claim?**

An interpretation does two things. It points to the evidence it rests on, by kind. It says what in that evidence shows the practice. A reader sees the evidence, or an excerpt of it, alongside the claim and the reason.

The interpretation is understandable to the learner and reviewable by others. It does not depend on an unexplained score.

### 4.5 Reporters

Whoever guided or observed the work in its context may make a report with a claim: a teacher, a mentor, a coach, a facilitator, or a family member. The learner may also make a claim about their own work. The report names who made the claim, as well as the role in which the claim was made.

## 5. Authorship and support

Help, collaboration, and AI use are not counter to agency. OMBS does not prohibit help. It asks whether the learner remained the source of choice. What is captured is the learner's authorship of intention, judgment, and learning, not whether every part was produced by hand.

Where polished artifacts are cheap, help can silently inflate every other practice. An artifact that performs well may be due to AI authorship. A tradeoff defended in writing may be copied from an AI chat. Authorship is what holds the rest of the standard honest. The hardest evidence to counterfeit is not a polished artifact. It is a coherent history of purposeful decisions, consequential iterations, informed reflection, and demonstrated understanding.

A helper may be a peer, a mentor, a family member, a kit, a code library, or an AI tool. The same three questions apply to all of them.

### 5.1 Direction

Who decided what to make and what to ask of the helper?

**Shows as:**

- What the learner asked of a person or tool traces back to the learner's own goal, and the learner can name what they chose not to hand off.

**Does not show as:**

- The learner asks "what should I make?" and builds the answer.

### 5.2 Judgment

How did the learner evaluate what the helper gave back, and what did they keep, change, verify, or reject, with reasons?

**Shows as:**

- A kept output counts, but only a reasoned keep. A silent keep, used with no recorded judgment, is a gap in the evidence, not a finding about the learner.

- The reason refers to the learner's own goal or audience, not the output's quality. "It was good" is not judgment. "It was too long for a six-year-old" is.

**Two errors this rules out:**

- Treating every change as evidence and every keep as nothing rewards pointless edits and mis-scores the learner who correctly judged a good output.

- Treating every keep as evidence scores the learner who accepted everything without looking.

### 5.3 Origin

Which parts of the work came from the learner, from a helper, from a helper and then revised by the learner, or from elsewhere, at a level a reader can check against the work?

- This is what makes the first two questions checkable. Without it the evidence ends in the learner's say-so.

- It is an evidence requirement, not an integrity rule. Whether undisclosed help breaks a rule is local policy.

**Shows as:**

- The learner can point at the work and show which part they made, which part a helper made, and which part a helper made and they then changed.

**Team projects:**

The contributions record who did what. This element records how each learner directed and judged the others' contributions to the parts they claim.

**Means of access:**

A tool used for access, such as speech-to-text, translation, or a screen reader, is not a contribution to the work and does not trigger these questions.

## 6. Research basis

We base our definition of learner agency and the approach for designing learning environment support of learner agency on the CAST Universal Design for Learning (UDL) Guidelines, available in full at [udlguidelines.cast.org](https://udlguidelines.cast.org). The goal of these guidelines is learner agency which is purposeful, reflective, resourceful, authentic, strategic, and action-oriented (CAST, 2024b). The OMBS report structure aligns with the UDL Guidelines, with further examples for the design of learning experiences using OMBS in our Design Guide. *See: Design Guide (in preparation).*

The outcomes of the UDL Guidelines have been studied across subjects and with students in K–12 through college, and this research demonstrates that UDL improves both student academic outcomes as well as student engagement. (CAST, 2026).

We base our definition of maker practices on practices described in the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013) and the International Technology and Engineering Educators Association (ITEEA, 2020). Both NGSS and ITEEA call for engineering design practices and concepts to be taught throughout the K–12 curriculum in order to build skills in problem-solving, creativity, and innovation (NGSS Lead States, 2013; ITEEA, 2020). We also extend these practices beyond engineering and science, drawing inspiration from creative making practices, in particular from Mitch Resnick’s book *Lifelong Kindergarten: Cultivating Creativity through Projects, Passion, Peers, and Play* (MIT Press, 2017). Other publications which influenced this work can be found in the bibliography section.
